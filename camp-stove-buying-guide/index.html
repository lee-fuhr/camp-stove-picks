How to choose a camp stove

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

The best camp stove for you depends on how you camp: for quick weekend car camping, a classic two-burner propane stove like Coleman’s Classic is hard to beat; for backpacking, an integrated canister system from Jetboil or MSR saves weight and boils fast; and for winter or international travel, a liquid-fuel stove gives reliable performance in cold temps. Focus on fuel type, burner output, and simmer control rather than gimmicks.

What type of camp stove is best for your trip?

Camp stoves generally fall into three categories: the familiar two-burner propane stove for car camping, the lightweight canister stove for backpacking or day hikes, and the liquid-fuel stove for serious expeditions. The two-burner type is roomy, stable, and cheap to run – great for cooking a full breakfast with bacon and eggs when you have a table and a cooler nearby. It’s heavy and bulky, so you won’t carry it far from the car. Canister stoves screw onto a small propane/isobutane tank and range from tiny screw-on burners that weigh next to nothing to integrated systems like the Jetboil Flash, which pots and burner as one efficient unit. They’re the go-to for any trip where weight matters – backpacking, bikepacking, or even a casual picnic hike. The catch: canister performance drops in cold weather or at very high altitudes, and the empties must be recycled properly. Liquid-fuel stoves (white gas, kerosene, or diesel) burn hotter in the cold and let you refill from a fuel bottle, so they’re the choice for mountaineering or long trips where you can’t rely on canisters. MSR’s WhisperLite Universal is a classic example. They require more maintenance, fuel spills can be messy, and they’re heavier than canister setups.

How many BTUs do you really need?

BTU ratings are everywhere, but they’re only a rough guide. A typical two-burner stove might claim around 10,000 to 12,000 BTUs per burner on high, which is more than enough to boil a pot of water for pasta in a reasonable time. But BTU numbers are measured differently by each manufacturer, so a 10,000 BTU burner from one brand might boil water slower than an 8,000 BTU from another. Wind, pot size, and fuel efficiency matter far more. For most car camping cooking, any standard two-burner stove will work fine. If you routinely cook for a large group and need to simmer multiple dishes, a stove with separate control valves and a wind guard will outperform a higher-BTU burner with no regulation. For backpacking, fast boil time is the key metric – many integrated stoves can bring two cups of water to a boil in under 3 minutes, which is more important than raw BTU numbers.

What about wind resistance and stability?

Wind is the silent killer of camp stove performance. A light breeze can cut heat output by 50% or more, turning a quick boil into a 20-minute chore. Look for stoves that include a built-in windscreen – many canister stoves come with a folding metal windguard, and some two-burners have a lid that flips to block drafts. You can always make your own windscreen from a piece of corrugated cardboard or a foil bake pan, but it’s safer to use one designed for the stove to avoid overheating the fuel canister. Stability matters just as much. A wobbling stove is a recipe for spilled dinner and burned fingers. Two-burner stoves with wide feet and a low center of gravity are best. For canister stoves, models with three wide-spaced legs or a flat base that accepts a tall pot are more secure. Many small stoves tip over if you use a large 8-inch pot full of water – that’s a common frustration. Read owner reviews that mention tipping or instability with specific cookware.

Can you simmer on a camp stove?

Simmering is the Achilles’ heel of many camp stoves. Most canister stoves have a simple on/off valve that makes low heat tricky – you turn the flame down and it suddenly drops from a roar to a sputter, or it flares up again. Two-burner propane stoves tend to have better simmer control because the valve is larger and you can fine-tune the flame. Some higher-end stoves, like the Coleman Triton series, have a separate simmer burner that’s designed for delicate cooking. If you need to make rice, oatmeal, or a sauce that requires gentle heat, look for a stove with a regulator that offers multiple heat levels. Integrated canister systems like the Jetboil Minimo include a simmer control ring that helps, but even then, simmering is never as easy as on a home gas range. For most campers, the solution is to master the “flame-off” technique – bring to a boil, then turn off the heat and let the residual heat finish the cooking. It’s not true simmering, but it works for many dishes.

How important are weight and packed size?

Weight and size dictate how you use a stove. A two-burner propane stove weighs anywhere from about 5 to 12 pounds and takes up a good chunk of a storage tub. That’s fine for car camping where you drive to the site. For backpacking, every ounce counts. A typical canister-top stove like the MSR PocketRocket 2 weighs less than 2 ounces and packs down to the size of a lemon. The trade-off: it’s not stable with big pots and has no windscreen. Integrated systems like the Jetboil Zip are heavier (around 7 ounces with the pot) but more fuel-efficient because the pot and burner are matched. If you hike in and out, the extra weight is worth it for the speed and convenience. For basecamp cooking where you carry gear a short distance from a vehicle, a mid-weight stove that balances portability and performance – say a single-burner butane stove with a sturdy base – can be a good middle ground.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use a camp stove in winter conditions?

Standard isobutane canister stoves lose performance below freezing because the fuel won’t vaporize properly. For winter camping, use a liquid-fuel stove (white gas) or a canister stove with an inverted canister design that lets liquid fuel flow. You can also warm the canister using your body heat, but that’s a temporary fix.

How do I clean a camp stove?

After each trip, wipe down burners and grates with a damp cloth and mild soap. For clogged burner jets on liquid-fuel stoves, use the cleaning tool that came with the stove or a thin wire. For canister stoves, avoid getting water into the valve. Store it dry. For two-burner stoves, regularly check the manifold for spiderwebs – they love to nest there and block the gas.

Is it safe to use a camp stove inside a tent?

No. Never use any camp stove inside a tent or fully enclosed shelter. Even a “low-CO” stove still produces deadly carbon monoxide, and the risk of setting the tent on fire is too high. Cook outside, under a tarp if needed, but always with ventilation.

Which camp stove is best for backpacking?

The best backpacking stove is the lightest setup that meets your needs. For solo trips, a screw-on canister stove like the MSR PocketRocket Deluxe gives you fast boil and good simmer control. For a group of two to three, an integrated system like the Jetboil Flash saves fuel and time. If you need to cook gourmet meals with several pots, consider a remote canister stove with a wider burner for even heat.

What is the best budget camp stove?

For car camping, the Coleman Classic two-burner propane stove offers reliable performance at a very low price point. For backpacking, the BRS-3000T is an ultralight canister stove that performs well for under a couple of hours’ wages. Both are proven designs, but budget stoves often lack simmer control and may have flimsy pot supports.

How do I know if a stove is compatible with my cookware?

Most camp stoves work with standard pots and pans, but stability and heat transfer depend on the burner size. A large pot (10 inches or more) can wobble on a small backpacking stove. For boil-only adventures, smaller pots are fine. For pan-frying, a stove with a wider burner grates and better heat distribution is best. If you use titanium cookware, note that it heats unevenly – aluminum or steel is more forgiving.

In shortChoosing the right camp stove is about matching the tool to your trip style, not chasing the highest specs. A simple two-burner propane stove is all most car campers will ever need, while backpackers should invest in a lightweight canister system with decent simmer control. And if you plan trips in truly cold or remote conditions, a liquid-fuel stove is a dependable partner. Base your decision on how and where you cook, and you’ll have many happy meals under the stars.
